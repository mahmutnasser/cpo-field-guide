Chapter 01

The interconnect wall

Training a frontier model means thousands to hundreds of thousands of accelerators acting as one machine. They exchange gradients and activations constantly, so the network decides how much of the expensive compute actually gets used.

Every link in an AI data center has to balance four things: bandwidth, reach, energy and cost. Copper wins on cost and reliability over short distances. As the signal rate per wire rises, copper loses strength faster at high frequencies, so its usable reach shrinks with every speed step. At 200 Gb/s per lane, passive copper cables reach roughly one to two metres; at 224G-class electrical signalling, studies put it under one metre41. Beyond that, the link has to be optical.

The industry splits AI networking into three domains, and you will see these words in every vendor presentation:

  • Scale-up: a small group of GPUs (72 to over 1,000) wired so tightly that they behave like one giant GPU with shared memory. Nvidia's NVLink is the dominant example. Today this is mostly copper inside one rack.
  • Scale-out: thousands of those groups connected through switches, over Ethernet or InfiniBand. This is already optical and is where CPO ships first.
  • Scale-across: linking separate halls or whole data centers kilometres apart, using longer-reach and coherent optics.

Why power is the real driver

Data centers are now limited by how much electricity they can get, more than by land or money. Any watt spent on moving bits is a watt not spent on compute. TechInsights estimates that pluggable transceivers alone would draw more than 2.5 GW across AI data centers by 2030 if the architecture did not change26.

A quick example makes this concrete. A 102.4 Tb/s switch filled with 64 pluggable 1.6T modules at roughly 25 to 30 W each spends 1.6 to 1.9 kW on optics. That can exceed the switch chip itself. Multiply by thousands of switches and every GPU's network ports, and you get tens of megawatts.

CPO attacks the part of that power that does no useful optical work: driving electrical signals across centimetres of circuit board, and cleaning them up again with a DSP. Broadcom reports about 70% lower optical interconnect power for its 51.2T CPO generation compared with pluggables33. Nvidia claims up to 5× lower power for its CPO switches34.

Specialist note · energy accounting, and why "pJ/bit" needs a definition

pJ/bit equals watts divided by Tb/s, so a 16 W 800G module is 20 pJ/bit and a 6.4 Tb/s engine at 32 W is 5 pJ/bit. Vendors draw the boundary differently:

  • Is the host SerDes power counted? In a pluggable link, the ASIC-side long-reach SerDes is often excluded, which flatters the module figure.
  • Is the laser counted at optical output or at wall-plug? External CW lasers at 20 to 30% wall-plug efficiency can add 1 to 2 pJ/bit.
  • Is FEC counted, and is the link one-way or round trip?

When you compare numbers in a pitch deck or paper, normalise to "end-to-end, host SerDes to host SerDes, including laser wall-plug and FEC". That is the figure a hyperscaler's power budget actually sees.

Chapter 02

The building blocks of an optical link

Every optical link, whether in a pluggable module or a co-packaged engine, does the same job: turn a fast electrical signal into modulated light, carry it, and turn it back.

Light sources

Silicon cannot emit light efficiently, so lasers come from III-V semiconductors, mostly indium phosphide (InP). Pluggables use EMLs (a laser with a built-in electro-absorption modulator) or DFB lasers. Silicon photonics and CPO use continuous-wave (CW) lasers that just shine steadily, with a separate modulator on the chip. For CPO, a few high-power CW lasers (300 to 800 mW class) feed many channels, and they usually sit outside the hot package in replaceable front-panel modules called ELSFP. Short multimode links use cheap VCSELs.

Modulators

The modulator imprints data on the light. The main types are the Mach-Zehnder modulator (MZM: robust and broadband, but long and power-hungry), the micro-ring modulator (tiny and efficient, but extremely temperature-sensitive) and the electro-absorption modulator (EAM). New materials push speed higher: thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) reaches over 110 GHz bandwidth with drive voltages below 3 V45,46, and barium titanate (BTO) and polymers are in development.

Waveguides, couplers, fiber

On-chip, light travels in silicon or silicon-nitride waveguides. ST's PIC100 platform, for example, reports losses around 0.4 dB/cm in silicon and 0.5 dB/cm in silicon nitride12. Getting light from a micron-sized waveguide into a 9 µm fiber core is done with grating couplers (surface-emitting, easy to test on wafer, narrower bandwidth) or edge couplers (broadband and lower loss, but harder to align and test). Data-center links mostly use single-mode fiber in the O-band around 1310 nm, where fiber dispersion is near zero.

Signalling: lanes, PAM4, SerDes, FEC

A lane is one independent channel. Today's lanes use PAM4, four signal levels carrying two bits per symbol, so a 200G lane runs at about 106 gigabaud. The SerDes on the host chip creates and receives these signals, and forward error correction (FEC) adds redundancy so the receiver can fix bit errors. Wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) lets several lanes share one fiber on different wavelengths.

Specialist note · ring modulators, thermal drift and the materials question

Silicon's thermo-optic coefficient is about 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, so a silicon micro-ring resonance drifts roughly 70 to 80 pm/K. Next to a switch ASIC whose local temperature can swing by tens of kelvin, that is many linewidths. Rings therefore need heaters and closed-loop locking, which costs power (often 1 pJ/bit or more) and control complexity.

Approaches being explored: overcladding with a material of negative thermo-optic coefficient (TiO₂ is the classic example, around −1 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹) to make the ring athermal; more efficient heaters with thermal isolation trenches; and MZM or TFLN designs that are less sensitive to temperature. This is directly relevant to a team with ALD TiO₂ capability (see chapter 10).

Other open materials questions for CPO: low-loss SiN for passive routing and couplers, hermetic or near-hermetic passivation of InP lasers and facets, and adhesives and index-matching materials that survive reflow and years at 85 °C or more.

Specialist note · a short-reach link budget

For a 500 m DR-type link at 200G/lane, a typical allocation looks like this: transmitter launch power a few dBm per lane; fiber loss about 0.2 dB over 500 m; two to four connectors at 0.3 to 0.5 dB each; fiber-to-chip coupling at each end of 1 to 3 dB depending on coupler type; plus penalties for dispersion, reflection and modulator extinction ratio. Connector count and cleanliness usually dominate over fiber length in short-reach links17.

CPO changes the budget: the laser now sits metres away in an ELSFP, adding a connector and fiber in the laser path and splitting one laser across several channels. That is why vendors push laser power toward 400 to 800 mW19, and why coupling loss on the PIC is the critical number.

Why not just keep improving pluggables?

Pluggables keep improving and will dominate unit volume for years. Arista's XPO multi-source agreement, announced in March 2026, packs 12.8 Tb/s into one liquid-cooled pluggable with a cold plate rated to 400 W20. LPO and LRO remove the DSP and save around 5 W per module32. Customers also prefer to push copper and pluggables as far as they go before switching, because they are simpler to deploy and repair36.

The limit is physics at the front panel. Every doubling of lane rate shortens how far the electrical signal can travel across the board, and the faceplate only has so much room for ports. At 400G per lane and switch chips of 200 Tb/s and more, the board trace itself becomes the problem. CPO removes the trace.

Two different CPO markets

Switch CPO (scale-out) puts optical engines around an Ethernet or InfiniBand switch ASIC. It is shipping now from Nvidia and Broadcom. Accelerator CPO / optical I/O (scale-up) puts optics next to GPUs and XPUs so that hundreds of them can share one memory domain across racks. That is the larger long-term prize and is where Ayar Labs, Lightmatter, Celestial AI (now Marvell) and Nvidia's NVLink roadmap are aimed.

Specialist note · EIC/PIC integration options

An optical engine combines an electronic IC (drivers, TIAs, control) with a photonic IC (modulators, detectors, couplers). The integration options, in rough order of performance:

  • Side-by-side with wire bonds: cheap, but bond-wire inductance limits speed.
  • Flip-chip with micro-bumps: the common approach today.
  • 3D hybrid bonding: copper-to-copper bonds at micron pitch. TSMC's COUPE stacks a 6 nm EIC on a 65 nm SOI PIC using SoIC-X, and claims about 40% lower power than micro-bump assembly at the same speed7.
  • Monolithic: electronics and photonics in one process (for example GlobalFoundries Fotonix, used by Ayar Labs). Simpler assembly, but the transistors lag leading-edge logic.

The structural point: the PIC stays on a mature node (45 to 65 nm is enough for optics) while the EIC moves to advanced nodes. That node mismatch is why heterogeneous bonding is winning.

CPO and optical circuit switching

Optical circuit switching (OCS) often appears next to CPO in the news, but it solves a different problem. CPO changes where the light is converted. OCS changes how the network is wired.

A CPO switch is still an electrical packet switch: light comes in, becomes electrical, the chip reads the address on every packet, and the result goes back out as light. An OCS never converts the signal at all. It steers light from one fiber port to another, like railway points, and keeps that path until it is told to change. It does not read packets.

That makes the two complementary. CPO lowers the cost of each conversion; OCS removes whole layers of conversions by replacing electrical switch tiers with passive light paths, and lets operators rewire the network in software. Google built its Jupiter data-center network this way: MEMS-mirror OCS units replaced the spine layer of its switch fabric, and Google reports 40% less power and 30% less cost for the network overall compared with the best alternatives it considered51,52.

Specialist note · OCS technologies and why loss matters even more
  • 3D MEMS mirrors (Google Apollo): two arrays of tilting micro-mirrors map any input fiber to any output. Large port counts, low loss, broadband; millisecond reconfiguration. Suited to topology changes, not per-job switching.
  • Silicon photonic switch fabrics: meshes of Mach-Zehnder or micro-ring elements, thermo-optic (microseconds) or electro-optic (nanoseconds). Compact and fast, but loss and crosstalk grow with the number of stages, so port count is limited.
  • SOA-gated switches: semiconductor optical amplifiers act as fast gates and offset loss, at the cost of noise and power.
  • Liquid-crystal and robotic patch panels: slower, used for reconfiguration and automation of fiber plants.

Because an OCS never regenerates the signal, every dB in waveguides, crossings, couplers and facets is subtracted from the transceivers' link budget at both ends. A path through a fabric may cross several stages and two fiber-to-chip interfaces. Low-loss SiN routing, sub-dB couplers and stable, low-reflection facet coatings are therefore even more valuable in OCS than in CPO, which is one more market for the materials work described in chapter 10.

Chapter 05

State of play, October 2026

CPO left the lab in 2025–26. One analyst summed up OFC 2026 as "Nvidia on one side and everyone else on the other"22. These are the moves that define the field now.

Nvidia: CPO in production, and buying the laser supply

Nvidia announced Quantum-X Photonics (InfiniBand) and Spectrum-X Photonics (Ethernet) at GTC 2025. Quantum-X offers 144 ports of 800 Gb/s with liquid cooling; Spectrum-X configurations reach about 100 Tb/s, for example 128 × 800G1. In July 2026 Nvidia's networking head confirmed CPO is in manufacturing for scale-out, with end products reaching the market this year23.

In March 2026 Nvidia invested $2 billion each in Lumentum and Coherent, including multibillion-dollar purchase commitments and capacity rights for laser components6. It has also invested in Scintil Photonics, a French company developing multi-wavelength lasers for scale-up CPO39. The lesson: the bottleneck has moved upstream to InP lasers.

At GTC 2026 Nvidia laid out the next step. The Feynman platform (2028) brings co-packaged optics into the NVLink scale-up switch, enabling NVL1152 systems: 1,152 GPUs across eight Kyber racks connected by optical NVLink4,35. Copper and CPO scale-up versions will coexist.

Broadcom: third-generation CPO, merchant silicon

Broadcom has shipped Tomahawk 6 Davisson since October 2025: a 102.4 Tb/s Ethernet switch with 16 optical engines of 6.4 Tb/s, 200G lanes and field-replaceable ELSFP lasers2,3. As a merchant vendor, Broadcom sells to everyone who is not buying Nvidia, and at OFC 2026 it also launched the first 400G-per-lane optical DSP, the 3 nm Taurus18.

TSMC: the foundry for optical engines

TSMC's COUPE platform enters volume production in 2026, with Nvidia and Broadcom named as first customers; a second generation follows around late 2027 to 20289,44. TSMC expects silicon photonics to exceed half of the optical transceiver market by 20278. Samsung Foundry is targeting a turnkey CPO offering around 20299.

The scale-up startups and their acquirers

  • Celestial AI was acquired by Marvell for about $3.25 billion upfront. Its Photonic Fabric chiplet offers 16 Tb/s for scale-up interconnect10.
  • Ayar Labs raised a $500 million Series E in March 2026 at a $3.75 billion valuation, with Nvidia and AMD participating11. Its TeraPHY UCIe optical chiplet delivers up to 8 Tb/s at roughly 5 pJ/bit37.
  • Lightmatter pursues photonic interposers and optical engines such as the 6.4 Tb/s Passage L20 for near-package use40.
  • AMD acquired the photonics startup Enosemi in 2025.

Europe

STMicroelectronics moved its PIC100 silicon photonics platform, co-developed with AWS on 300 mm wafers in France, into high-volume production in 2026 and plans to quadruple output by 202713. ST also published a through-silicon-via roadmap aimed at near- and co-packaged optics12. In the Netherlands, PhotonDelta funds the integrated photonics ecosystem around Eindhoven and Twente and is a partner in the European PIXEurope pilot line.

The market numbers, and why they disagree

Forecasts vary widely depending on what counts as CPO. LightCounting (cited by ST) expects data-center pluggables to exceed $34 billion by 2030 with CPO adding more than $9 billion13. TechInsights projects total transceiver revenue growing from $9.7 billion in 2025 to $84.3 billion by 2030, with CPO near $30 billion26. TrendForce sizes the AI optical-transceiver market at about $26 billion in 202642. Investors will quote whichever suits their thesis; quote the range and your own bottom-up logic.

Practical advice

Join the conversation early. OIF and OCP membership is affordable for a startup, and the working groups are where you meet the engineers who write purchase requirements. A component designed to an MSA has a market; a component that needs a custom interface has a single customer at best.

Chapter 07 · addendum

What the IEEE Heterogeneous Integration Roadmap adds

The 2025 edition of the IEEE/SEMI/ASME roadmap has a Photonics chapter (Chapter 9, Rev 0.9, March 2026) that covers CPO, III-V integration and fiber attach59,60. It is a technology-assessment document, not a market forecast, so it complements the company-led timeline above. Most of it agrees with this guide; the useful part is where it puts the weight.

Where it confirms the guide

  • Power: CPO saves roughly 20% more than direct-drive LPO, and Broadcom’s 51.2T switch is quoted at up to 70% below pluggables. Reported system energy is about 6.6 to 7 pJ/bit incl. laser for Broadcom and Cisco switches, below 3 to 5 pJ/bit for optical I/O chiplets (Intel, Ayar Labs), and about 2.4 pJ/bit for Celestial AI.
  • Remote lasers: keeping the laser away from the hot ASIC extends its life, and makes it replaceable, which is why external laser sources keep appearing in the standards table.
  • Standardisation is unresolved: the OIF 3.2T CPO module agreement was not implemented because its assumed architecture gave no power or cost benefit, and there is no consensus on what to standardise without constraining the architecture. The 2026 MSAs in chapter 06 look like the industry’s answer.

Where it adds something

  • Thermal is the top red-flag barrier. Silicon and InP resonant devices drift about 0.08 and 0.1 nm per °C, while the switch ASIC next door can exceed 500 W. Thermistors do not work well on dense chips, dummy heaters cost power, and no good thermal-electrical-optical co-simulation tools exist. The proposed direction is micro-TECs at specific spots (TIPS). My reading: this is where an athermal material, such as the negative thermo-optic coefficient of TiO₂ noted in the glossary, deserves a hard look.
  • Packaging, not the PIC, drives cost. Packaging and fiber attach often cost more than the photonic chip itself. Self-aligning optics (±30 µm tolerance, about 0.5 dB), laser fusion splicing, photonic wire bonding and ML-assisted alignment (from 30 to 60 s down to under 5 s per fiber) are the routes; the roadmap names fiber attach below $0.10 per port as a 2030 to 2035 target. Teramount, Photonect, MicroAlign and Vanguard Automation are cited as startups here.
  • Laser integration is a supply-chain gap. Micro-transfer printing places III-V devices at about ±0.5 µm (3σ) but at roughly 10,000 dies per hour, and it has no known-good-die test. LIFT, borrowed from micro-LED displays, exceeds 8 million transfers per minute there, but photonics needs about ten times tighter alignment than displays (±0.5 µm versus ±5 µm). Foundry access for III-V on silicon is currently limited to Tower. imec, Tyndall and UGent hold the most advanced MTP capability, and the EU PhotonixFAB project is building service access.
  • Reliability bar. Optical engines inside a switch cannot be repaired in the field, so their FIT rates must match the ASIC’s. Wafer thinning to about 10 µm, TSV stress that shifts bandgap-sensitive devices such as EAMs and photodetectors, ESD exposure at die-to-die interfaces and copper contamination are the named failure risks. JEDEC JC-14.3 is writing SiPh qualification. Intel reports FIT below 0.1 across 32 million integrated lasers.
  • Light sources. Membrane lasers on SiC exceed 110 GHz bandwidth (97 GHz at 55 °C, 74 GHz at 85 °C); quantum-dot lasers on silicon have passed 100,000 hours at 35 °C and could remove the isolator; heterogeneous lasers still run at only about 10% wall-plug efficiency. A low-loss integrated isolator and monolithic III-V on silicon remain red barriers.

Its three horizons

  • 2025 to 2030: CPO at scale with 12.8T and 25.6T engines, MTP in volume manufacturing, wafer-level PIC packaging cutting per-port assembly cost by about an order of magnitude, 1.6T transceivers reaching 10 million units a year.
  • 2030 to 2035: sub-2 pJ/bit optical I/O, 3D electronic-photonic stacks as standard practice, multi-vendor CPO module interfaces, multi-foundry III-V-on-silicon services, 32+ channel membrane laser arrays.
  • 2035 to 2045: monolithic III-V lasers on silicon, 100 Tb/s-class PICs, sub-0.5 pJ/bit inter-chiplet optics.

Chapter 08

The hard problems

At SPIE Photonics West and OFC in 2026 the message was consistent: optical performance is mostly solved; integration, manufacturing and operations are not30. Each problem below is also a market.

Fiber attach and coupling Critical

Aligning dozens of fibers to a PIC at sub-micron accuracy, at volume, without active alignment. The industry has not converged on one architecture, and LightCounting called it one of the biggest open problems23.

Detachable connectors, passive alignment features, mode converters, low-loss edge couplers, AR and index-matching coatings.

Yield and known-good die Critical

A failed optical engine can scrap a package that holds a very expensive ASIC. Silicon photonics has higher process variation than CMOS and limited rework30.

Wafer-level optical test, burn-in of engines before assembly, process monitoring, defect metrology.

Thermal environment High

Optics sit next to a kilowatt-class chip. Lasers degrade with heat, and ring resonators drift by tens of pm per kelvin. Most CPO systems are liquid-cooled28.

Athermal designs, thermal isolation, efficient heaters and control loops, materials with negative thermo-optic coefficient.

Serviceability High

Operators lose the hot-swappable module. Lasers moved to ELSFPs; Open CPX now tries to make engines socketed and replaceable21.

Sockets, connectors, field diagnostics that tell contamination from damage.

Laser supply High

High-power CW InP lasers are scarce; Nvidia spent $4 billion to secure capacity6.

Laser facet passivation and coatings, reliability testing, alternative sources (comb lasers, heterogeneous III-V on Si).

Test and qualification High

Production test needs automated fiber connection and full optical characterisation before the engine meets the ASIC29. Hyperscaler qualification takes 12 to 36 months.

Probe cards with optical access, test services, reliability labs.

Advanced packaging capacity Medium

CPO competes with GPUs and HBM for TSMC's CoWoS and SoIC lines41.

Alternative assembly flows, outsourced assembly and test (OSAT) partners in Europe.

Ecosystem and lock-in Medium

Nvidia ships a vertically integrated stack; everyone else depends on MSAs that are still forming. Choosing the wrong standard or failing a customer qualification is a real risk42.

Standards-compliant components that serve several camps at once.

Copper incumbency Medium

Customers push copper to its limit first. Co-packaged copper and new cable designs keep extending copper inside the rack36.

Target links where copper physically cannot follow: multi-rack scale-up and 400G lanes.

Chapter 10

Where a small photonics team fits

The giants own the switch chips, the foundry and the system. Startups win by solving one hard problem in their stack better than anyone, in a form they can qualify and buy. This chapter applies that to a three-person team with photonics research depth, nanomaterials and fabrication patents, ALD TiO₂ capability and access to TU/e facilities in Eindhoven.

A possible sequence

  1. Start with A. A coating and passivation service generates early revenue, puts you inside customers' engineering teams, and produces reliability data you could not get otherwise.
  2. Build B on that access. Use customer feedback and TU/e facilities to develop athermal or low-loss TiO₂ processes, file patents, and validate them on real PICs from a foundry multi-project wafer.
  3. Exit or scale through partners. License to foundries and engine makers, or become the specialist materials supplier that an engine maker or foundry acquires.

Optical sensing remains a useful hedge. The same thin-film and PIC know-how sells into LiDAR, spectroscopy and biosensing, which have shorter qualification cycles than hyperscale data centers.

Questions to answer in the first 90 days

  • Which failure modes do engine makers and laser suppliers actually see near the ASIC: facet degradation, moisture, outgassing, ring drift? Who pays to fix them?
  • What coupling loss, back-reflection and temperature range must a coating or cladding meet, and how is it qualified (Telcordia GR-468-style testing or hyperscaler-specific)?
  • Is ALD TiO₂ compatible with the thermal budget and contamination rules of TSMC, GF, ST or imec back-end flows?
  • How much heater power per ring could athermal cladding save, in pJ/bit, at the system level?
  • Who is the first buyer: a foundry, an engine maker, a laser maker, or an OSAT? What volume and price would they commit to?
  • What does the patent landscape for athermal claddings and facet passivation look like?

European resources to look into

  • PhotonDelta: the Dutch integrated photonics programme on High Tech Campus Eindhoven, with funding, network and a contribution of about €54 million to the European PIXEurope photonics pilot line47.
  • TU/e: photonic integration research with roughly 300 researchers49, cleanroom access, and existing data-center spin-offs such as PhotonX Networks48.
  • EU programmes: EIC Accelerator (grant plus equity for deep tech), Chips Joint Undertaking pilot lines, and regional Brainport programmes. Check current calls and eligibility before planning on any of them.
  • Investors active in Dutch photonics include PhotonDelta's own fund and deep-tech funds such as Innovation Industries.
Specialist note · why TiO₂ is interesting here, and what could go wrong
  • Negative thermo-optic coefficient. TiO₂ (amorphous or anatase) has dn/dT of roughly −1 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, opposite in sign to silicon and SiN. A cladding of the right thickness can cancel the ring's thermal shift over a temperature window. This is published in academic literature; the open question is manufacturability.
  • High index and low loss. Index around 2.2 to 2.5 makes TiO₂ useful for compact passive structures, AR stacks and mode converters.
  • ALD conformality. ALD gives uniform, pinhole-free films on high-aspect-ratio features and facets, suited to passivation and moisture barriers (often in TiO₂/Al₂O₃ nanolaminates).
  • Risks. Crystallisation of TiO₂ above roughly 300 to 400 °C changes index and loss; film stress; hydrogen content; and whether a foundry will accept a new material late in its flow. Each needs early data.

Challenges and opportunities, in one place

Everything in the field guide that matters for building a company in co-packaged optics, gathered on one page: the forces working for and against a new entrant, fourteen specific openings in the value chain, the business obstacles, and the deals that show what investors and acquirers pay for.

Tailwinds
  • CPO is shipping in volume switches from Nvidia and Broadcom, so demand for its components is real23,2
  • Cloud capex near $830 billion in 2026, mostly for AI27
  • The bottleneck has moved to components: lasers, fiber attach, test6,23
  • Open multi-source agreements are opening the engine layer to more suppliers21
Headwinds
  • A handful of buyers with enormous bargaining power
  • Qualification cycles of one to three years
  • Giants are integrating vertically and locking in suppliers
  • Copper and pluggables keep lasting longer than forecast36
Where small teams win
  • Solve a problem every camp shares, instead of betting on one architecture
  • Sell to the suppliers of the giants first
  • Build a data moat: reliability and yield data take years to collect
  • Be easy to acquire: chip companies are buying photonics teams

Part D

Checklist before choosing an opening

Answer these with real conversations, not desk research, before committing time and money to one of the openings above.

  1. Who pays, and how much? Name the first buyer, the price they would accept, and the volume they could commit to.
  2. Which metric moves? Translate the benefit into pJ/bit, dB of loss, yield, reliability or cost per Gb/s (see chapter 4 of the field guide).
  3. Does it work in every camp? Check that the product fits Nvidia, Broadcom and open-standard designs, and pluggables too.
  4. Can it be qualified? Find out which tests the buyer requires and how long they take.
  5. Is it compatible with the process flow? Confirm temperature limits, contamination rules and materials accepted by the target foundry or assembly line.
  6. Is there freedom to operate? Search patents before building, and decide what you will file.
  7. What is the cheapest proof? Define a first demonstration that runs on shared facilities and multi-project wafers.
  8. Who would acquire it? List the three companies most likely to buy the company, and check they buy companies like it.
